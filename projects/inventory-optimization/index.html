The business question

Inventory parameters were not consistently aligned with demand patterns, lead-time risk, or item criticality. Treating every item the same tied up working capital and obscured which materials needed tighter protection.

The goal was to make the next decision clearer, not simply produce another report.

How I approached it

I began by separating the inventory problem into two questions: which items were consuming working capital, and which items could not be allowed to disrupt operations? Looking only at value would have encouraged broad reductions, while looking only at service risk would have protected too much stock.

To balance both sides, I segmented items by annual usage value, demand behavior, criticality, and lead-time exposure. I reviewed excess, obsolete, slow-moving, and shortage-prone materials within those groups, then tested changes to reorder points, safety stock, and order quantities. Recommendations were prioritized where the carrying-cost opportunity was clear and the effect on material availability could be understood rather than assumed.

What I delivered

The analysis produced an ABC and demand-variability segmentation, an inventory-health view, planning-parameter recommendations by item class, and a targeted action list for excess and shortage-risk material. This gave decision makers a defensible explanation for why one item should be reduced, another protected, and a third monitored before any change was made.

Business impact

The analysis identified practical parameter and disposition changes that delivered approximately $30K in annual carrying-cost savings while keeping service risk visible in the decision.

What I learned

Inventory optimization is not a blanket reduction exercise. The strongest recommendation separates material that is expensive from material that is operationally critical.
