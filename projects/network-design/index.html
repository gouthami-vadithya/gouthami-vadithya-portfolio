The network question

A growing product network needed to decide how many distribution centers to operate and where to place them. The lowest fixed-cost option increased delivery distance, while the highest-service option added capacity and facility cost.

The best network was not the cheapest scenario. It was the scenario with the strongest total tradeoff.

How I modeled it

I began by organizing customer demand, facility capacity, transportation rates, service expectations, and disruption risk into one network view. The lowest-cost configuration was not automatically the strongest option because a facility decision could lower transportation expense while weakening delivery coverage or concentrating too much risk in one location.

I created alternative facility and flow scenarios, calculated their cost and capacity implications, and compared how each option performed under normal demand and disruption conditions. The recommendation was developed from the full tradeoff between operating cost, customer service, capacity headroom, and resilience rather than from one favorable metric.

Deliverables

The academic project produced a demand-and-capacity map, transportation-cost model, facility scenario comparison, service-coverage view, and a recommended future-state network. The decision summary made the logic transparent by showing what the preferred design improved, what it cost, and which risks would still require mitigation.

Modeled impact

The selected scenario offered a stronger balance of service coverage and capacity without adopting the most expensive network. All outcomes are modeled for academic purposes.

Tradeoffs made visible

The recommendation explained not only which scenario performed best, but why the alternatives were rejected.

What I learned

Network decisions are long-term commitments. A good model should make assumptions and risk visible so leaders understand how the answer changes when demand, cost, or service priorities shift.
