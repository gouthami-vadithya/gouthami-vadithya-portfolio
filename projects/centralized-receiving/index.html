The business question

Three locations handled deliveries differently. Inconsistent intake, documentation, and exception handling made it harder to confirm receipt, trace responsibility, and resolve missing or damaged material.

The goal was to make the next decision clearer, not simply produce another report.

How I approached it

I started by following the receiving journey at each of the three locations, from the SAP Ariba purchase order and delivery arrival through inspection, documentation, internal distribution, and final receipt confirmation. Speaking with the people performing the work exposed where local practices had developed and where material could lose its owner, evidence, or status during a handoff.

I mapped those differences before designing the future state because a single workflow would only work if it accounted for real operating conditions. I then built one standard path for normal deliveries and separate exception paths for damaged, missing, unidentified, or time-sensitive material. Responsibilities, evidence requirements, escalation points, and system updates were defined at every handoff so the process could be followed consistently without relying on personal knowledge.

What I delivered

The resulting operating model included current-state and future-state process maps, a receiving checklist, an exception and escalation matrix, and clear role ownership. Together, these materials gave all three locations the same language for receiving work while still making room for location-specific conditions that required an explicit rule.

Business impact

The design gave three locations a shared operating language and a clearer path from delivery to confirmed receipt. It reduced ambiguity in the process and made exception ownership explicit.

What I learned

Standardization works only when the future-state process reflects how work actually moves at each location. Local differences need an explicit rule, not an informal workaround.
