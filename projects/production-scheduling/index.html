The operations problem

The original plan loaded work based on due date alone. That approach pushed too much work through one constrained workstation, increased waiting, and made the final schedule look achievable on paper but unstable in execution.

The schedule was not failing everywhere. It was failing at one constraint that controlled the rest of the flow.

Analysis and decisions

I started by translating the operation into the constraints that made a schedule feasible: available capacity, setup time, job precedence, labor limits, and customer due dates. Rather than optimizing an unrealistic plan, I first established what each resource could actually complete and where work would compete for the same capacity.

I built an initial schedule, tested the bottleneck, and then compared sequencing and capacity scenarios. When a change improved one measure but created lateness elsewhere, I traced that tradeoff through the full schedule before accepting it. The final recommendation combined a feasible sequence with clear triggers for overtime, rescheduling, or capacity escalation.

Deliverables

The project resulted in a capacity model, constraint map, production schedule, bottleneck analysis, and scenario comparison. A management view summarized utilization, late-order risk, and the decisions required when demand exceeded the available plan. Because this was an academic case, the results are presented as modeled operational outcomes rather than employer performance.

Modeled impact

The optimized scenario produced a more stable schedule with less queue buildup at the constrained resource. Results are based on an academic model and are not presented as employer performance.

Feasible before fast

The project showed that a realistic schedule creates more value than an aggressive plan the operation cannot execute.

What I learned

Improving the entire system starts with understanding the constraint. Local efficiency can make total flow worse when it creates more work in front of the bottleneck.
